The River That Could Not Speak

The moment I saw the river, I knew something had gone terribly wrong..... The river behind my grandmother's village had always reflected the palm trees like a long green mirror. Now grey foam covered the water, and a sharp smell rose from beneath the bridge. Plastic bottles were trapped among the reeds. My younger cousin Faris stood beside me, unable to speak. "What happened to our river?" he finally asked.

We followed the footpath to a narrow channel carrying cloudy water from the direction of a nearby workshop. The sight looked , although we had no proof of where the dirty water came from. Pak Din, a fisherman, was sitting beside his unused boat. "It has been getting worse for weeks," he told us. He had been to report it because several villagers worked at the workshop. "But if we stay quiet, the water may further," he added.

I listened in silence. Until then, I had assumed that environmental problems were caused only by big companies or careless strangers. However, the plastic caught in the reeds reminded me of the snack wrappers I sometimes dropped beside overflowing bins at school. I always believed that one wrapper was too small to matter. Suddenly, the truth hit me: the river had been damaged by many small choices, including mine. I wanted to blame someone else because that was easier than admitting I had also been careless.

Faris and I photographed the channel and rubbish without going near the water. With Pak Din's help, we sent the pictures and his observations to the local council so officials could . A week later, our school organised a clean-up along the safe, dry part of the riverbank. We collected bottles and bags while teachers reminded us not to touch the river. I made a poster explaining how rubbish and waste could people and animals living nearby. Working beside my classmates, I realised that taking felt better than complaining.

Three weeks later, the river had not returned to its old colour, but less rubbish lined the banks, and officials were checking the channel. Pak Din watched us from beside his boat. "You cannot save a river in one afternoon," he said, "but you can stop making it worse." I looked at the water that had frightened me on my first visit and understood what he meant. Real change would take time, but it had already begun with our small actions. As I walked home, I realised that the river could not speak for itself, but I had finally learnt not to remain silent.
